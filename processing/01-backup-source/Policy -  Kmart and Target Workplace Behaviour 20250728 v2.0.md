Policy – Workplace Behaviour

INTRODUCTION
AND PURPOSE

Kmart and Target (together referred to as the Company) are committed to diversity, equal opportunity and promoting a safe and healthy environment in our workplaces. Unacceptable workplace behaviour, including unlawful discrimination, harassment (including sex-based harassment), sexual harassment, grooming for sexual conduct with a child or young person, bullying, victimisation, vilification, hostile work environments on the ground of sex or any other inappropriate behaviour will not be tolerated.

The purpose of this Workplace Behaviour Policy (Policy) is to:
• communicate and promote the standards of behaviour expected of all Company employees (Team Members) and all contract/agency workers (including labour hire workers), agents, consultants, apprentices, volunteers, vocational and work experience placements, suppliers of the Company and all other persons conducting a business or undertaking for or on behalf of the Company (Workers);
• define and prevent unacceptable conduct or behaviour that is inappropriate and/or unlawful;
• outline how concerns under this policy can be raised and the support available; and
• outline the consequences of breaching this policy.

Team Members and Workers are required to comply with their obligations under this Policy. If you breach an obligation placed on you in this policy, the Company may take disciplinary action against you. Disciplinary action may include termination of your employment or engagement with the Company.

POLICY APPLICATION
This Policy applies to all Team Members and Workers.

This Policy applies to the workplace including, but not limited to:
• conduct in the workplace and in situations where work is performed away from the workplace;

1. APPROPRIATE
WORKPLACE BEHAVIOUR

We all have a role to play in ensuring that the culture and behaviour in our workplace is aligned to the Company values. As such, we all have a responsibility to behave in accordance with this Policy, treat everyone fairly, with dignity and respect and:

• Think customer – We put the customer at the heart of everything we do
• Take Care – We look after ourselves, our teams, customers, contractors and our community
• Live Integrity – We always do the right thing
• Grow Together – We achieve the best results by working together
• Reach Higher – We are bold and relentless in delivering improvements both big and small

All Team Members and Workers have a legal responsibility to care of their own health and safety and the health and safety of others, whilst at work. Part of this responsibility means that Team Members and Workers must not engage in, or condone, discrimination, harassment, sexual harassment (including sex-based harassment), bullying, victimisation, vilification, grooming for sexual conduct with a child or young person, work environments that are hostile on the ground of sex or any other inappropriate workplace behaviour in any way.

We want everyone to feel safe and supported and we will not tolerate behaviour/conduct that is a breach of this Policy.

This means that everyone must report behaviour or conduct, which is in breach of this Policy or that makes them feel uncomfortable or is inappropriate. No matter how big or small and no matter whether it has happened to you directly or you have witnessed it, we want you to speak up. Please refer to Appendix 1 which details how you can report behaviour or conduct that is in breach of this Policy.

3. LEGISLATIVE FRAMEWORK
This Policy is intended to reflect relevant legislation in Australia, which means that breaches of this Policy may also be against the law and some breaches of this Policy may constitute a criminal offence.

Under the relevant legislation (identified in section 16), it is not just the individuals directly involved in a breach who can be held liable. 4. DISCRIMINATION

Unlawful discrimination occurs when a person treats or proposes to treat a person or groups of people unfavourably, because of a personal attribute, as compared to someone who does not have that attribute. Set out below are examples of protected attributes that may be applicable under Commonwealth, State and Territory legislation:

• Age.
• Race (including colour, descent or national or ethnic origin).
• Pregnancy, potential pregnancy or breastfeeding.
• Identity of spouse or domestic partner.
• Marital, relationship or domestic partnership status.
• Family responsibilities/parenthood and carer's responsibilities.
• Gender identity/gender history/transgender, including intersex status/sexual characteristics.
• Sexual orientation/sexuality.
• Sex and lawful sexual activity.
• Human Immunodeficiency Virus ('HIV') or Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome ('AIDS') status.
• Disability/impairment (including physical, intellectual or psychiatric) or genetic predisposition to disability.
• Physical features.
• Lawful industrial activity (e.g. membership or non-membership of a union).
• Religion or religious belief, conviction or activity.
• Political opinion, belief, conviction, affiliation or activity.
• Irrelevant or spent criminal record.
• Irrelevant medical record.
• Employment status and employment activity.
• Family or domestic violence.
• Accommodation status (e.g. periods of homelessness or not having a fixed address).

It is also unlawful to discriminate or harass a person based on their association with another person, identified by one or more of the listed protected attributes. Legal action can be taken against individuals who breach this legislation, as well as against the Company.

Please note that a person can unlawfully discriminate against another person, even if they did not intend to do so.

Discrimination can either be 'direct' and/or 'indirect'.

Direct discrimination occurs when a person is treated less favourably because of a protected attribute.

Examples of direct discrimination in employment include:
• Refusing to employ a person because of their race.
• Refusing to promote someone because of their sexuality.
• Not providing opportunities to a person because they are pregnant.
• Dismissing a person because of their age.
• Making offensive 'jokes' or comments about another person's racial or ethnic background, sex, sexual preference, age, disability and the like.
• Displaying pictures, calendars, pin-ups, posters, computer images (e.g. in email) etc. that are offensive or derogatory.
• Expressing negative stereotypes of particular groups.
• Judging a person on characteristics, such as religious or political beliefs, cultural practices, sex or age, rather than work performance.
• Using stereotypes or assumptions when making decisions about a person's career.
• Making an employment decision based on the sexual preference or religious belief information that a person has posted on social media.
• Not allowing a transgender person to use the bathroom facilities appropriate for their current gender.

Direct discrimination in the provision of goods and services may include situations where a customer of:
• a particular ethnic origin or race is refused service or is served in a different way, compared to others; or
• a certain race or age group is subjected to more stringent security requirements (e.g. a person is required to undergo a bag check because of the colour of their skin).

Indirect discrimination occurs when a requirement (e.g. a practice, policy or procedure) has or is likely to have the effect of disadvantaging certain groups because of a protected attribute and the requirement is unreasonable in the circumstances or where a high proportion of people, because of a protected attributed, cannot comply.

5. HARASSMENT

Harassment is any uninvited, unsolicited or unwelcome behaviour, which offends, humiliates or intimidates another person or group of persons (i.e. harassment can occur even when the conduct is not directed at a person, but rather witnessed by a person – e.g. a person overhears offensive jokes).

Harassment on the basis of a ground of discrimination, such as sex, age, marital status, race or disability is unlawful.

In harassment cases, the intention of the alleged harasser is irrelevant. What is important is what a reasonable person would think of the situation. Harassment can be a single or repeated act of offensive behaviour and the person being harassed does not first need to ask for the behaviour to stop.

Examples of harassment

Harassment may include physical, visual, written, verbal or non-verbal conduct. Examples include, but are not limited to:

• Racially oriented verbal abuse.
• Displays of offensive material, for example posters or pictures.
• Irrelevant references to a person's racial, cultural, ethnic or religious background or affiliation.
• Offensive jokes, gestures or comments (including on online forums or social media where there is a direct link to work) about another person's racial or ethnic background, sex, sexual preference, age or disability.
• Asking questions about a person's sexual preference or activities.
• Mimicking someone's accent, or the habits of someone with a disability.
• Continual and unwarranted shouting in the workplace.
• Phone calls or messages via social media or email which are threatening, abusive or offensive.

6. SEX-BASED HARASSMENT

Sex-based harassment is unlawful and occurs where a person harasses another person based on that person's sex (including a characteristic that relates to or is imputed to that person's sex), by engaging in unwelcome conduct of a demeaning nature and where a reasonable person, having regard to all the circumstances, would have anticipated the possibility that the person harassed would have been offended, humiliated or intimidated.

It is also unlawful and prohibited to subject a person to a hostile workplace environment on this same basis. The circumstances to be taken into account when determining whether a person has subjected another to a hostile workplace environment on the ground of sex includes, but is not limited to, the following:
• the seriousness of the conduct;
• whether the conduct was continuous or repetitive; and
• the role, influence or authority of the person engaging in the conduct.

Conduct such as displaying obscene or pornographic materials, general sexual banter or innuendo and offensive jokes could contribute to a sexually hostile workplace and result in unlawful discrimination, such as sexual harassment.

Examples of sex-based harassment

Examples of sex-based harassment include:
• Asking intrusive personal questions, based on a person's sex.
• Not hiring a person based on assumptions that they will not fit the workplace based on their sex.
• Making inappropriate comments and jokes to a person, based on their sex.
• Displaying images or materials that are sexist, misogynistic or misandrist.
• Making sexist, misogynistic or misandrist remarks about a specific person.
• Requesting a person to engage in degrading conduct, based on their sex.

7. SEXUAL HARASSMENT

Sexual harassment is unlawful and occurs when a person engages in unwanted or unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature, and a reasonable person, having regard to all the circumstances, would have anticipated the possibility of making the other person or persons feel offended, humiliated or intimidated.

Conduct of a 'sexual nature' includes:
• Subjecting a person to any act of physical intimacy.
• Making, orally or in writing, any remark or statement with sexual connotations to a person or about a person.
• Making any gesture, action or comment of a sexual nature.

In relation to allegations of sexual harassment, the intention of the alleged harasser is irrelevant. What is important is whether a reasonable person would have anticipated the possibility that the other person would be offended, humiliated or intimidated by the conduct. It is important to be aware that what is acceptable to one person may not be acceptable to another.

Sexual harassment is considered serious misconduct and it represents a valid reason for dismissal. It is also prohibited under both Federal and State legislation, and legal action can be taken against individuals who breach the legislation.

Examples of sexual harassment

• Unwelcome sexual advances and requests for sexual favours (including making promises or threats in return for sexual favours).
• Unwelcome comments or questions about a person's sex life or physical appearance.
• Suggestive behaviour, such as staring or leering at a person or parts of their body.

• Offensive telephone calls, mobile telephone SMS or picture text messages, letters, facsimiles or electronic messages, including emails.
• Displaying sexually offensive photographs, reading material, graffiti, screensavers, internet material or objects.
• Sexual propositions or continued requests for dates.
• Repeated unwanted gifts, such as flowers or cards.
• Unwelcome physical contact, such as touching, grabbing, hugging, pinching or kissing.
• Sexually explicit conversations.
• Making obscene gestures.
• Actual or attempted indecent assault, stalking or rape.

Some of these forms of sexual harassment are also criminal behaviour and may be treated as a criminal offence.

What is not sexual harassment?

Sexual harassment is not behaviour which is based on mutual attraction, friendship and respect that is invited, consensual or reciprocated. However, in these circumstances, it is still important to be mindful that a conflict of interest may arise.

A conflict of interest exists where the individual's personal interests (these include the individual's personal interests, as well as those of third parties, including family, persons with whom the individual is in a relationship, friends or associates) may conflict with their responsibility to act in the best interest of the Company and their ability to remain impartial could be compromised (e.g. a personal relationship between a direct report and a line manager). All Workers and Team Members have an obligation to declare any real, potential or perceived conflict of interest to their Line Manager, so that it can be assessed and managed.

8. WORKPLACE BULLYING

Workplace bullying is repeated, unreasonable behaviour, directed towards a worker or a group of workers that creates a risk to health and safety (including psychological, emotional or physical health).

'Unreasonable behaviour' is that which a reasonable person, having regard to all the circumstances, would see as unreasonable, including behaviour that is victimising, humiliating, intimidating or threatening.

Repeated behaviour refers to the persistent nature of the behaviour. Repeated behaviour does not necessarily refer to repeated instances of the same type of unreasonable behaviour. A pattern of behaviour involving a series of different types of unreasonable behaviour may constitute bullying.

Bullying can lead to serious breaches of occupational health and safety laws, claims under the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) and can also be prosecuted under common law.

Bullying involving threatened or actual physical violence can also lead to the possibility of criminal charges. For example, in Victoria, bullying can also amount to stalking, which is a criminal offence.

Examples of bullying

Examples of bullying behaviour include:
• Aggressive and intimidating conduct.
• Abusive, insulting or offensive language or comments.
• Belittling or humiliating comments.
• Unjustified criticism or complaints.
• Deliberately excluding or isolating someone from workplace events or activities.
• Withholding information that is vital for effective work performance.
• Setting unreasonable timelines or constantly changing deadlines or creating unreasonable work expectations.
• Setting tasks that are unreasonably below or beyond a person's skill level.
• Spreading misinformation or malicious rumours.
• Practical jokes or unreasonable initiation practices.
• Changing work arrangements, such as rosters and leave, to deliberately cause inconvenience.

Workplace bullying does not include:
• Reasonable management action, carried out in a reasonable manner. This may include the following:
    o Setting reasonable performance goals, standards and deadlines.
    o Rostering and allocating working hours, where the requirements are reasonable.
    o Transferring a worker for operational reasons.
    o Deciding not to select a worker for promotion where a reasonable process is followed.
    o Informing a worker about unsatisfactory work performance in an honest, fair and constructive way.
    o Informing a worker about inappropriate behaviour, in an objective and confidential way.

9. VICTIMISATION

It is unlawful and against this policy to victimise a Team Member or Worker for making a complaint under this policy, equal employment opportunity or OHS legislation. Such conduct can be the basis for civil action (in addition to a criminal conduct).

Victimisation occurs where a person is subject to intimidation (e.g. threats or 'pay-backs') or any other adverse treatment or detriment, because they:
• assert their rights under this policy (e.g. making or assisting a person in making a complaint), or any relevant legislation.
• give evidence in response to a complaint (e.g. a witness in a workplace investigation);
• refuse to discriminate or harass another person; or
• exercise a workplace right.

'Detriment' in an employment setting includes demotion, dismissal, transfer, suspension, loss of a benefit, being ostracised from work or work-related functions or being the subject of gossip or innuendo.

Examples of victimisation

Examples include, but are not limited to the following:

• Giving a warning to someone for being a witness to a complaint of race discrimination made by a colleague.
• Being denied a promotion or being moved to a position with lower responsibility, after helping a colleague make a sexual harassment complaint.
• Dismissing someone for making a discrimination complaint.

11. VILIFICATION

Vilification is a public act, which incites, encourages or urges others to hate, have serious contempt for or severely ridicule a person or group of people on the grounds of (among other things):
• Race.
• Religion.
• Sexual orientation or sex.
• HIV/AIDS status.
• Transgender.
• Disability.

A person does not need to belong to a particular group, but instead could be thought to be a member of it in order to be vilified.

Vilification is unlawful and has no justification in 'free speech'. Vilification may also breach Federal and/or State legislation. Legal action can be taken against individuals who breach this legislation and may amount to a criminal offence.

Examples of vilification

Examples include, but are not limited to the following:
• Inciting other people to threaten harm towards other people or their property.
• Hate-speech including on websites, online or in material.
• Wearing or displaying racist material in public.

12. GROOMING FOR SEXUAL CONDUCT WITH A CHILD OR YOUNG PERSON

Grooming for sexual conduct with a child or young person under the age of 16 years is a criminal offence that targets predatory conduct undertaken to prepare a child or young person for sexual activity at a later time.

Grooming is a criminal offence in most States and Territories in Australia and will not be tolerated.

The offence applies where an adult communicates, by words or conduct, with a child or young person under the age of 16 years or with a person who has care, supervision or authority for the child or young person with the intention of facilitating the child or young person's involvement in sexual conduct, either with the groomer or another adult.

The offence can be committed by any person aged 18 years or over. It does not apply to communication between people who are both under 18 years of age.

Examples of grooming behaviour:
• giving gifts or special attention to a child or young person, or their parent or carer, making the child or young person feel special and/or indebted to an adult;
• making close physical contact sexual, such as inappropriate tickling and wrestling/play fighting;
• openly or pretending to accidentally expose a child or young person to nudity, sexual material and sexual acts (this in itself is classified as child sex abuse but can also be a precursor to physical sexual assault);
• controlling a child or young person through threats, force or use of authority making the child or young person fearful to report unwanted behaviour.

Grooming may not necessarily involve any sexual activity or even discussion of sexual activity – for example, it may only involve establishing a relationship with the child or young person, or their parent or carer, for the purpose of facilitating sexual activity at a later time.

Any Team Member or Worker who witnesses or becomes aware of grooming in the workplace must report it (please refer to Appendix 1).
